What GrowthLens measures, and how much to trust it

GrowthLens starts from one number the state already computes for every student and turns it into a school-by-school reading of where a district is beating expectations, where it is falling short, and where the gaps between student groups are widest. This note explains what those readings are built from, the judgment calls behind them, and where they stop being reliable.

1 · What the numbers mean

Everything in GrowthLens rests on a single figure the state calculates for each student, a growth score. It answers a narrow but useful question: did this student learn more, less, or about as much as other students who started the year in the same place? Zero is a typical year of growth. A positive score is ahead of that expectation; a negative score is behind it. The state estimates this for us — taking account of where each student began and what grade they are in — and reports it on a standard scale. The unit is the standard deviation (SD), the natural yardstick for how spread out the scores are; most students land within one SD of the middle. GrowthLens does not recompute it. Our work begins afterward: combining these student scores into larger figures, and being clear about how far each one can be trusted.

From that one growth score, GrowthLens builds three views at widening scale — a single school or grade, the gap between two groups inside a school, and the district as a whole.

How a school is growing

Average the growth scores of a school’s students — or of a single grade within it — and you learn whether that group gained more or less ground than expected over the year. This is the raw material for the school-by-grade map.

The gap between two groups

Take two groups of students in the same school — students from low-income families and their classmates, say — and subtract one group’s average growth from the other’s. That difference is what we call a gap. Two of its features are easy to miss. It is a gap in growth, not in achievement: a school can have a wide achievement gap and a narrow growth gap, or the reverse, and the two say different things. And because both groups are measured against the same expectation inside the same school, the gap does not depend on how that school compares with the rest of the state. We always write a gap as the focal group minus the comparison group — low-income minus not-low-income, students with disabilities minus those without, and so on — so a negative gap means the first group grew less.

A single figure for the district

A district wants one number, not forty. The district-wide gap is an average of the individual schools’ gaps, but a deliberately uneven one: a gap measured on four hundred students says more than one measured on forty, so better-measured schools count for more. Next to the figure we report a range — the values that remain plausible given the data. That range widens for two distinct reasons, and it pays to hold both in mind: each school’s gap is itself uncertain, and the schools genuinely differ from one another. When the range comfortably clears zero, the district has a real, consistent gap; when it straddles zero, the honest reading is that we cannot yet tell.

2 · Steadying the small schools

A school of thirty students should not move the district’s picture the way a school of six hundred does — its number is mostly noise. So GrowthLens pulls each school’s estimate partway toward the district average, and pulls hardest on the schools it knows least about. Statisticians call this shrinkage, or partial pooling — a formal name for an old habit of mind.

How far a school is pulled comes down to a contest between two things — how precisely its own gap was measured, and how much the district’s schools truly differ from one another. A large, cleanly measured school barely moves. A small, noisy one moves a long way. We report the size of the pull as a number between 0 and 1 — the app labels it B, shown when you hover a school on the Group gaps by school page and as the “Shrinkage B” column in the table: 1 means the school’s own data was taken at face value, 0 means it was set aside entirely for the district average. Most schools sit well inside that range and keep the bulk of their own signal.

Pulling a school toward the district also narrows the range around it — the school borrows a little certainty from its neighbors. You get a steadier number, but only by accepting that it has been moved toward the district average.

Steadying a school can make its gap look bigger, not smaller. A school whose own gap is smaller than the district’s gets pulled toward that larger district number, so its reported gap widens. A school measured at exactly zero, in a district whose gap is −0.20, will be reported a little below zero. The model has not invented a problem out of nowhere; with little data of its own, the school’s most likely true value lies somewhere between its noisy estimate and the district norm. The smaller the school, the more you should read the range rather than the single point — and the pull figure tells you exactly how far the number was moved.

Sometimes a district’s schools simply cannot be told apart on a given comparison: the differences among them are no larger than the measurement noise. There is then nothing real to steady toward, and forcing the calculation would paint every school at the district average with false confidence. When that happens, GrowthLens stops steadying and shows the schools’ own unadjusted numbers instead, and says so on the page.

3 · Two kinds of uncertainty

When one school’s gap differs from another’s, there are two possible reasons, and telling them apart is the whole game. The first is that we measured a finite number of students, so some difference would turn up by chance even if the schools were identical. The second is that the schools really are different. GrowthLens estimates how much of the spread is genuine and how much is noise, because that balance is what decides how hard to steady each school and how wide to draw each range.

The genuine school-to-school spread — what is left once the measurement noise is accounted for — is the figure the district overview labels How much schools really differ (the between-school SD). When it is small next to the typical measurement error, the schools are mostly indistinguishable and the apparent differences are largely chance; when it is large, the schools really do differ, and the ordering means something. It is not the spread of the numbers you see plotted, which run wider than the truth before steadying and narrower after.

Two smaller modeling choices round this out. First, the state gives us a measurement error for every individual student score — uncommon in data like this — and we use it as a floor. If a school’s few scores happen to land close together, the raw spread can understate how noisy the underlying measure really is; rather than claim more precision than the test can deliver, we hold each school’s uncertainty to at least what its students’ own measurement errors imply. Second, schools whose groups fall below the minimum size (next section) are left out when we estimate the genuine spread and the district average, since their gaps are too noisy to inform either — though they still appear in the figures, steadied like the rest. Dropping that noisiest tail can pull the estimated spread a little low, which we accept: better to understate real differences than to manufacture them.

4 · When a group is too small to trust

Below some number of students, a school-and-group result tells you almost nothing, and a chart that shows it anyway invites the reader to over-read noise. GrowthLens flags any group with fewer than 10 students in this version and sets those results apart from the trustworthy ones rather than blending them in. A configurable threshold is planned, so a district will be able to match its own privacy and small-cell rules.

Flagged schools are not dropped — that would hide a school from its own district and quietly redraw the picture. Instead they sink to a labeled Too few students to read reliably group at the bottom of the school-by-school plot, dimmed so they stay in view without being mistaken for results you can lean on. The district-wide figures, though, are built only from schools that clear the threshold (see §3), so a handful of noisy small groups can sit on the page without dragging the headline number around.

A caution if these results ever leave your screen: ten is a sensible bar for working through your own data, but it is not a publication standard. Many state report cards suppress any cell below thirty students. Apply the stricter rule before anything goes public — the threshold here is a working tool, not a disclosure-control rule.

5 · Weeks of learning

“A tenth of a standard deviation behind” means nothing to most people; “a few weeks behind” means something instantly. So every figure offers a second way to read its numbers — an SD / Weeks switch in the sidebar that restates the same difference in weeks of learning, roughly how many weeks of a typical school year’s progress it represents. The standard scale is the unvarnished one, the difference exactly as the model sizes it; weeks put the same number in language a school board can use. The switch only relabels the axis — it never changes which schools look strong or weak.

Where the conversion comes from

To turn a standardized difference into weeks, we need to know what a year of learning is worth on the same scale, and we take that from the state’s own published results. Comparing this year’s average fifth-grader with last year’s average fourth-grader — two snapshots of roughly the same students, a year apart — gives a year of typical growth, which we put on the standard scale and then divide the school year into. A school year runs about 38 instructional weeks, so:

weeks = 38 × (the growth difference) ÷ (a typical year’s growth, same scale)

A student a tenth of a standard deviation ahead of expectations, in a grade where a year of growth is about a third of a standard deviation, is therefore roughly ten weeks ahead. The conversion factors — one for each grade, subject, and year — are built from the published statewide figures and ship with the app. Where a single figure spans several grades, we average the grades’ growth rates first and convert once; doing it the other way around would inflate the weeks — by a few percent when growth is fairly even across the grades, by a third or more when one grade’s growth is far slower than the rest.

Weeks add no new information; they only relabel the standard-deviation figure in friendlier units. The comparison behind the conversion is of different students a year apart, so shifts in who is enrolled leak into the figure, and it assumes learning accrues evenly across the year. Worse, growth itself differs by grade. Where a typical year’s growth is small, an ordinary difference converts into a startling number of weeks. A middle-grades reading gap of a fifth of a standard deviation can read as well over a year of “weeks” — not because the gap is enormous, but because a year’s growth in that grade is small to begin with. When a weeks figure looks dramatic, check it against the standard-scale value beside it. Weeks are meant to convey scale, not to schedule instruction.

Comparing average scores across adjacent grades only means something if the grade-to-grade scale was built to support that comparison. It is the same assumption behind the widely used national growth benchmarks of Bloom and colleagues (2008), which rest on exactly this adjacent-grade design, and our factors show the pattern familiar from that work: large gains in the early grades, tapering through the middle grades. And because the conversion uses statewide growth, a district that truly grows faster or slower than the state will get weeks that are internally consistent but scaled to the state’s pace rather than its own.

6 · The two main views

Two views carry the analysis, in a natural order. The school-by-grade grid comes first — it answers where to look. The group-gaps view comes second, once a particular gap is worth chasing — it answers whether that gap is everywhere or sits in a few buildings.

Group gaps, school by school

For one subject and one pair of groups, this shows a row per school: the school’s gap, the range around it, and how far it was steadied toward the district. The district-wide gap is drawn as a gold dashed line running straight down through the rows (with a matching gold diamond on the District row pinned at the top), so you can see at a glance which schools fall short of it and which clear it. Reach for it when you want to know whether a gap is a district-wide pattern or the work of a few schools.

Growth by school & grade

A grid of schools against grades, shaded from blue (faster growth than expected) to rust (slower), with an overall column. Start here, before drilling into any single gap. It shows at a glance where growth is consistently strong or weak, and whether a pattern runs across the district — a soft fourth grade everywhere, say — rather than sitting in one building.

7 · What GrowthLens is not

GrowthLens is built to sharpen the questions a district asks of its own data. It is just as important to be plain about what it cannot do:

  • It does not evaluate teachers. The growth scores account for where students started, but nothing here isolates the effect of any particular teacher, classroom, or program. These numbers cannot support judgments about individuals and should not be used for them.
  • It does not explain why. The figures show where gaps and slow growth appear; they are silent on the cause. A gap tied to family income folds in everything that travels with income that the state’s model does not adjust for.
  • It is not a state report card. Suppression rules differ from state to state. Check yours before anything is published.
  • It trusts the state’s growth scores. If a district’s real pattern of growth is badly mismatched to the state’s model — strong nonlinearity, heavy mid-year mobility — the scores can mislead, and GrowthLens has no way to detect it. Treat it as a tool for triage, not a final word.

8 · The cutoffs behind the written summaries

The written summaries on the cards and in the export have to decide when a difference is worth a sentence — when to call a group “ahead,” when to flag a cell as a standout. Those calls run on the fixed cutoffs below. They govern emphasis, not statistical significance — the ranges above handle that — and they are editorial choices, not findings, listed here so you can argue with them.

CutoffWhat it does
±0.02 SDDirection words (“ahead”/“behind”) and gap-reversal mentions stay silent inside this band — smaller differences are treated as noise in prose.
0.05 SDThe fastest-vs-slowest-grade line needs at least this much spread between grade means before it appears.
0.15 SDA school-and-grade cell is called a “standout” at or beyond this size.
2/3Share of the schools with enough students that must lean the district’s way before a gap is called district-wide rather than concentrated.
2 × gapWithin-group spread must exceed twice the between-group gap before the “groups overlap heavily” line appears.
−0.05 SDThe Resources page flags a grade band as growing below expectations when its average growth — with bigger schools counting for more — falls at or below this.

9 · Privacy

Your data never leaves your browser, and you can prove it yourself: open your browser’s developer tools, watch the network panel, and load a file. The file is read and analyzed on your own machine — no student record, summary, or partial result is sent anywhere. Even the engine that does the analyzing is served from the same place as the app, not fetched from some other server, so opening a file makes no outside request at all. Close the tab and the data is gone; GrowthLens stores nothing between sessions.

(When the page first loads, its code and fonts come from standard web sources, as any site’s do. Your data is never part of that.) The full code and methods are public, at andrewmcamp/growthlens.
