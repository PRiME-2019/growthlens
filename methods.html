How GrowthLens estimates school performance, gaps, and uncertainty

A short, honest tour of the math behind GrowthLens — written for district leaders, data analysts, and curious skeptics alike. Here's what we compute, where the numbers come from, and the assumptions that tell you how hard you can lean on them.

1 · What is being estimated

Before the formulas: GrowthLens is built from three simple pieces — a residual (growth compared with what was expected), a gap (the difference between two student groups at the same school), and one district-wide number that pulls the school-level gaps together.

The residual

In plain terms, a residual is growth compared with what was expected: did a student grow more, less, or about as much as the state model predicted? For each student in a given subject, GrowthLens reads in a standardized growth residual that the Missouri DESE / MOSIS growth model has already worked out — the difference between the student's observed score and the score the state model predicts from prior achievement and grade, already expressed in standard-deviation units (the state's Z_RESIDUAL). GrowthLens does not fit this residual model itself; it takes the residuals the state supplies, then aggregates and shrinks them. A school × grade × subgroup cell gathers these standardized residuals together.

The gap

A gap is simply the difference between two student groups at the same school. More precisely, it's the within-school difference in mean residual between two subgroups, signed focal − reference — for example FRL minus non-FRL, so a negative gap means the focal group (FRL students) has the lower mean residual. It is not a difference in raw achievement, and it is not a difference relative to the state — both subgroups in a gap are measured against the same district baseline. That's why the district-wide gap can look small even when raw achievement disparities are large.

The district-wide number

The single district-wide gap is an average across schools that gives steadier, better-measured schools more weight — a random-effects pooled mean over the per-school estimates, weighted by the inverse of each school's combined sampling and between-school variance:

w_i = 1 / (SE_i² + τ²) μ = Σ w_i · r_i / Σ w_i SE(μ) = 1 / √(Σ w_i)

Here r_i is the school's raw gap, SE_i is its within-school standard error, and τ² measures how much schools really differ from one another — the between-school variance component (see §3). The 95% confidence interval shown on the overview card — the range of values consistent with the data at 95% confidence — is μ ± t·SE(μ), where t is the Student-t critical value with k − 1 degrees of freedom and k is the number of schools in the fit. With few schools the usual 1.96 is too narrow because τ² is itself a noisy estimate; the t correction restores honest coverage (with 30+ schools, t ≈ 1.96). One notational simplification throughout: we write μ for the estimate computed from your data — the quantity statisticians would top with a hat.

2 · Shrinkage (empirical Bayes)

The idea here is simple: a result built on a handful of students shouldn't swing the whole picture, so we gently nudge each school's number toward the district average — more for small, noisy schools and less for large, well-measured ones. Statisticians call this shrinkage, and the recipe below is the classic empirical-Bayes approach (it learns the right amount of nudging from the data itself). School-level estimates are shrunken toward the district-wide gap, so the shrunken estimate at school i is a precision-weighted average of the school's raw estimate and the district mean:

B_i = τ² / (τ² + SE_i²) ← shrinkage factor r_i_shrunk = B_i · r_i_raw + (1 − B_i) · μ

The shrinkage factor B_i tells you how far a school was nudged toward the district average, on a scale from 0 to 1:

  • B_i = 1 — the school's raw data is fully trusted; the estimate stays exactly at the raw value. This happens only in the limit of zero within-school sampling error.
  • B_i = 0 — the school's data is set aside entirely in favor of the district mean. You approach this when the within-school SE is much larger than the between-school SD.

Most schools in a typical district land between 0.6 and 0.9. On the Group gaps by school page, the hover tooltip shows each school's B on the chart, and the Table view includes it as a column.

The shrunken estimate's standard error folds in the uncertainty about the district mean itself: SE(r_i_shrunk) = √( B_i · SE_i² + (1 − B_i)² · SE(μ)² ). Intervals are then built with the Student-t critical value at k − 1 degrees of freedom rather than 1.96, because μ and τ² are themselves estimated from only k schools. Two degeneracy guards apply: when fewer than two schools clear the minimum-n threshold, or when τ² is estimated at exactly zero (the schools are statistically indistinguishable on this comparison), shrinkage would pin every school to the district mean with a zero-width interval — a fabricated certainty — so GrowthLens reports the raw estimates instead (B = 1).

These choices are calibration-checked by simulation: across districts of 5–30 schools and realistic heterogeneity, the displayed 95% intervals cover the true school effect roughly 92–98% of the time, deliberately erring conservative in the smallest districts. The simulation scripts ship with the source (tools/validate-stats-*.cjs) and can be re-run with plain node.

The same machinery runs at three grains, each shrinking toward its natural pool: school-level gaps and overall growth shrink toward the district-wide value, and the heatmap's school × grade cells shrink toward their grade's district-wide mean — within a grade column, schools are the exchangeable units. The demographics box plots are deliberately not shrunken: they display observed student distributions, not model estimates, and rest on hundreds of students each.

Important and a little counter-intuitive. Shrinkage nudges every school toward the district mean — including schools whose raw gap is smaller in magnitude than the district average. A school with a raw gap of 0.00 SD, in a district whose gap is −0.20 SD, will end up with a shrunken estimate that is slightly negative. That's mathematically correct under the model — with limited data, our best guess for the school sits somewhere between its own (noisy) number and the district norm — but it can look as if the model "invented" a gap. When a school is small, lean on the credible interval (the range the real number most likely falls in) rather than the single point estimate. (The shrinkage factor B shown with each school says exactly how far its number was nudged.)

3 · Variance components and τ²

This section is about uncertainty — separating two kinds of "wiggle" in the numbers. Some spread comes from having only so many students in a school (sampling noise), and some comes from schools genuinely being different from one another. Telling these apart is what lets us shrink the right amount and draw honest intervals. τ² below is just our measure of how much schools truly differ.

The within-school cell standard error

The state hands us a per-student measurement SE s_i for each residual — something most pipelines don't have — and GrowthLens uses it as a precision floor without double-counting. For a cell of n students with sample variance S² and mean measurement variance ms² = (1/n) Σ s_i², the cell standard error is

SE_cell = √( max(S², ms²) / n )

When students in a cell genuinely vary (S² ≥ ms², the common case) this reduces to the classic √(S²/n). When a small or very uniform cell's sample variance understates the true noise, the state's measurement variance steps in as a floor, so we never claim more precision than the measurements support — and we never double-count measurement error, since it's already baked into S². A gap combines its two cells by adding their squared errors: SE_gap = √(SE_A² + SE_B²).

Between-school variance τ²

To find how much schools truly differ, we fit a model that splits the spread into a between-school piece and a within-school piece. The between-school variance τ² is estimated by REML — restricted maximum likelihood, a standard, well-behaved way to estimate that piece without underselling it — fit to the random-effects model

r_i = μ + u_i + e_i u_i ~ N(0, τ²) ← between-school e_i ~ N(0, SE_i²) ← within-school sampling

The between-school SD shown on the Overview card is τ = √τ². Think of it as how much schools really differ from one another — the spread of the true per-school gaps if the model is correct. It is not the spread of the observed point estimates, which runs larger (because of sampling error) for raw estimates and smaller (because of shrinkage) for shrunken estimates.

If τ² is small next to the average within-school SE², schools are mostly hard to tell apart: most of the apparent spread is just sampling noise. If τ² is large, schools really do differ.

Under the hood, τ² is found by a bounded one-dimensional maximization of the REML profile log-likelihood over τ² ≥ 0 (a golden-section search, anchored by the closed-form DerSimonian–Laird estimate — a fast, classic formula for this same quantity). Schools whose cells fall below the minimum-n threshold (§4) are left out of the τ² fit and the pooled mean — their gaps are too noisy to inform the variance component — but they still show up in the figures, shrunk toward μ. Dropping that high-SE tail can pull τ² slightly low; this is a known, documented limitation.

4 · Minimum-n threshold

Some groups simply have too few students to read reliably, and we flag those rather than letting them mislead you. Every cell (a school × subgroup cell on the Group gaps by school page, a school × grade cell on Growth by school & grade) is checked against a minimum sample size, n_min, which is fixed at 10 in this build. A configurable threshold is planned for a future release, so you'll be able to match it to your district's student-privacy and small-cell-suppression policies.

Threshold modeWhat it does
sectionBelow-threshold schools move to a labeled group at the bottom of the forest plot. This is the default.
inlineBelow-threshold rows keep their sorted position but are dimmed and tagged with an n* badge.
hideBelow-threshold rows drop out of the figure entirely. (In every mode, the district-wide statistics are fit only to schools that meet the threshold — see §3.)

If GrowthLens output is going to leave the analyst's workstation, apply a stricter standard before publishing — many state report cards use n ≥ 30 for public-facing tables. The default of 10 is fine for internal triage but is not a suppression rule.

5 · Weeks of learning

Every figure can show its numbers two ways: on the standard-deviation (SD) scale the state's residuals arrive in, or converted to weeks of learning — about how many weeks of a typical school year's progress the same difference represents. SD is the honest native unit, but few people outside measurement work carry an intuition for "0.10 SD"; weeks give the same number a familiar size. The toggle lives in the sidebar, and the two are exact rescalings of one another — switching units never changes which schools look strong or weak, only the words on the axis.

Where the conversion factor comes from

The factors are built from Missouri's own statewide MAP results — the grade-level means and SDs DESE publishes each year — one factor per year × grade × subject. For, say, grade 5 math in 2025, we ask: how much did the statewide average score rise between grade 4 in 2024 and grade 5 in 2025, measured in grade-4 SDs? That is a year of typical growth, as an effect size:

annual_growth_es = (mean_grade_g_year_t − mean_grade_g−1_year_t−1) / sd_grade_g−1_year_t−1 weeks_per_SD = 38 / annual_growth_es weeks = weeks_per_SD · residual

This pairing — this year's grade 5 against last year's grade 4 — is a synthetic cohort: two adjacent statewide snapshots standing in for one group of students followed over time. Dividing a typical Missouri school year (38 instructional weeks) by the effect size says how many weeks one SD of growth is worth; multiplying by a residual converts it. A student 0.10 SD ahead of expectations, in a grade where a year of growth is 0.38 SD, is about 38 · 0.10 / 0.38 ≈ 10 weeks ahead. The factor table ships with the app (reference/conversion_factors.json), and the script that builds it from the DESE workbooks is in the repository.

Which factor each figure uses

  • Per-grade cells — the school × grade heatmap converts each cell with its own grade's factor for the data's year and subject, since a year of growth is worth very different amounts in grade 4 and grade 8.
  • Pooled displays — school-level gaps, the district box plots, and the overview numbers span several grades, so they use the year × subject average across grades 4–8. Figure footnotes show the multiplier in use.
  • Grade 3 has no factor of its own — there is no grade-2 MAP to grow from — so grade-3 cells use the grade 4–8 average as well.
  • Missing years fall back to the nearest earlier year with factors. Statewide testing was cancelled in 2020, so neither 2020 nor 2021 can have a factor of its own (2021 lacks its prior-year baseline); data from those years converts with 2019's factors. If the factor table fails to load entirely, a typical MAP constant (≈132 weeks per SD) keeps the toggle working, and the footnotes say so.
Read weeks as a translation, not a second measurement. The synthetic cohort compares different students a year apart, so enrollment shifts between years leak into the factor. The arithmetic assumes growth spreads evenly across a 38-week year. And because annual growth differs sharply by grade — a typical year of middle-school ELA growth can run under 0.2 SD statewide — modest SD differences can convert to eye-catching week counts; that is the grade's small typical growth doing the talking, not a bigger underlying gap. Weeks are rounded to whole numbers and are meant for communicating how big a difference is — not for curriculum pacing or instructional-time decisions.

Assumptions worth knowing

Comparing scale-score means across adjacent grades assumes MAP's grade-level scales are articulated well enough for that difference to carry meaning — the same assumption behind the widely used national growth norms (Bloom, Hill, Black & Lipsey, 2008, Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness), which are built from the identical adjacent-grade design. Our factors show the familiar pattern from that literature: large annual gains in the early grades that shrink through middle school.

Pooled displays average the effect sizes first and convert second (38 / mean(es)). Converting each grade first and then averaging the weeks (mean(38/es)) would give a number roughly 20% larger — for 2025 ELA, 96 vs. 116 weeks per SD. GrowthLens uses the first form: it weights grades by their growth on the score scale and yields the more conservative conversion.

A worked extreme: in 2024, grade-6 ELA's annual growth was 0.13 SD — one SD is then worth about 295 weeks, so a grade-6 cell of +0.20 SD displays as roughly +59 weeks, over a year and a half of typical grade-6 ELA learning. That is the grade's small typical growth doing the talking, not a larger underlying difference; read such cells with the SD value alongside.

The fallback constant above (132 weeks per SD = 38 ÷ 0.29) uses a typical mid-grades annual-growth effect size consistent with the national norms cited here.

6 · The two figures

GrowthLens gives you two main views: one to drill into a single gap school by school, and one to scan the whole district at a glance. Here's how to read each.

Group gaps by school (forest plot)

One row per school for a single subject and subgroup pair, showing the point estimate, the 95% credible interval (the range the real number most likely falls in), and the shrinkage factor. The district-wide gap sits on top as a vertical reference line. Reach for this when you want to know whether a gap concentrates in a few schools or shows up across the whole system.

Growth by school & grade (heatmap)

A school × grade grid of standardized residuals, colored on a diverging scale around zero (blue above expectation, rust below). Start here before you drill into a specific gap — to spot schools and grades that are consistently above or below expectation, and to see whether the district shows a coherent pattern (say, 4th-grade weakness across the board).

7 · What GrowthLens is not

A few honest limits, so you know where to stop. GrowthLens is built to help you ask better questions — not to assign blame or hand down a verdict.

  • Not a value-added model. The state's residuals already account for prior score and grade, but GrowthLens estimates no teacher-, classroom-, or program-level effect. Don't use these results to evaluate individual teachers.
  • Not causal. The estimates show where gaps and residuals turn up. They don't tell you why.
  • Not a state report card. Cell-suppression rules differ from state to state, so cross-check before anything goes public.
  • Not bulletproof against a badly mismatched model. If a district's true growth pattern is wildly nonlinear, or leans hard on factors we don't include (like mid-year mobility), the residuals can mislead. Treat GrowthLens as a triage tool, not a final judgment.

8 · Fixed editorial thresholds

The plain-language takeaways and the Resources matcher use a handful of fixed constants to decide which sentences are worth writing. They are editorial — they choose emphasis, not significance (the interval-based gates described above are separate) — and are listed here so the choices are reviewable:

ConstantWhat it does
±0.02 SDDirection words ("ahead"/"behind") and gap-reversal mentions stay silent inside this band — differences smaller than this are treated as noise in prose.
0.05 SDThe fastest-vs-slowest-grade takeaway requires at least this spread between grade means before it appears.
0.15 SDA school × grade cell is called a "standout" at or beyond this magnitude.

9 · Privacy and data handling

The short version: your data never leaves your browser tab, and you can check that yourself. All computation runs right in your browser via DuckDB-WASM, which is vendored and served from the same origin as the app — so loading a data file makes no third-party or CDN request, and once the page itself has opened, the analysis needs no further network access. (The app shell — React and fonts — does load from CDNs when the page first opens; your data is never part of any request.) Your CSV is parsed and queried entirely in the tab; it's never uploaded to a server, and no identifier, aggregate, or partial result is sent off the device. Want to confirm it? Open your browser's developer tools and watch the Network panel — you'll see no off-origin traffic during analysis. Reload the tab and the data is gone — GrowthLens keeps nothing between sessions and stores no student records in cookies or local storage.

The published methods, fixtures, and code are at andrewmcamp/growthlens.
